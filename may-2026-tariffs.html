Tariffs Are Slowing Consumer Spending: What NNN Owners Need to Know About Their Tenant

Ryan Lundgren
May 11, 2026
4 min read

There's a number getting quietly passed around in real estate circles right now: 12%. That's the estimated average tariff rate on all U.S. imports as of early 2026, according to the Urban-Brookings Tax Policy Center. It sounds abstract until you start thinking about what it means for the retailers sitting inside your NNN property.

Here's the real issue. Most NNN investors underwrite the lease: the term, the rent bumps, the credit rating. What they underwrite less rigorously is the tenant's business. And tariffs are a business problem, not a lease problem. A lease doesn't protect you from a tenant whose margins are getting squeezed to the point where renewal looks optional.

Not all tenants are created equal here

The brands with the most exposure are those with supply chains deeply tied to foreign economies: discount retailers, apparel, home goods, and general merchandise. Dollar stores are a good example of the tension. They're expanding aggressively. Dollar General is opening 450 stores this year alone. But their entire value proposition is low price points on imported goods. If tariff costs get passed through to consumers, their core customer feels it first. That pressure is especially acute in secondary and tertiary markets where dollar store density is highest.

On the other side of the ledger, tenants insulated from tariff pressure share a few characteristics: domestically produced or delivered services, essential demand that doesn't compress with price sensitivity, and strong unit-level economics that give them room to absorb cost increases.

Auto service, QSR with domestic supply chains, medical services, and financial tenants generally fall into this bucket. These categories aren't immune to macroeconomic pressure, but their structural exposure to tariff-driven cost inflation is meaningfully lower.

The consumer backdrop matters too

As of Q1 2026, NNN investors face a mixed macro environment: tariff volatility is creating supply chain uncertainty, the Fed has been slower to cut rates than markets expected, and the question investors are increasingly asking isn't "will there be a recession?" but "which tenants in my portfolio will perform if conditions deteriorate?"

That's exactly the right question for property owners to be asking about their own asset.

The takeaway for NNN owners

If you're holding a NNN property and haven't thought about your tenant's tariff exposure, now is the time. The lease term on your document doesn't change, but the likelihood of a healthy renewal conversation in 3, 5, or 7 years is absolutely influenced by how well your tenant navigates the next 18 months. The owners who understand that distinction will make better decisions about when to hold, when to sell, and what their asset is really worth in today's market.
